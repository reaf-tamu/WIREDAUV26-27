## 1. Overview

The VN-100 is an **AHRS** — an Attitude and Heading Reference System. In plain terms, it's a small box that tells you which way something is pointing. Inside, it combines three separate types of sensors (see section 2) and does the hard math of turning their raw, noisy readings into one clean, continuous orientation estimate, entirely onboard its own processor — we never have to do that fusion ourselves.

This is our team's primary source of vehicle orientation: roll, pitch, and yaw, plus how fast the vehicle is rotating on each of those axes.

## 2. What it does and why we use it

**Sensing principle:** The VN-100 is built around three separate physical sensors, each measuring a different physical quantity, plus an onboard barometric pressure sensor:

- **Accelerometer** — measures linear acceleration on 3 axes (including gravity). On its own, this can estimate "which way is down," but it's easily fooled by the vehicle's own real acceleration (e.g. thrusters firing), and it can't sense rotation about the vertical axis at all.
- **Gyroscope** — measures angular velocity (how fast it's rotating) on 3 axes. Very responsive and immune to linear acceleration, but drifts over time if used alone — small errors accumulate the longer you integrate them.
- **Magnetometer** — senses Earth's magnetic field, giving an absolute compass heading. Good for correcting the gyroscope's long-term drift, but noisy and easily disturbed by nearby motors, thrusters, or ferrous metal.

No single one of these three sensors is trustworthy on its own for very long. The VN-100's real value is that it runs a sensor fusion algorithm **internally**, continuously blending all three (a textbook real-world use of the same idea covered in `docs/concepts/kalman-filter.md`) to produce one orientation estimate that's more stable and accurate than any single sensor could give alone. We receive the *already-fused* result — we never touch the raw magnetometer/gyro/accelerometer fusion math ourselves.

**How this compares to other terms you'll hear:**

These terms get used loosely and interchangeably in casual conversation, but they mean genuinely different things:

- **IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit)** — the base-level term. Strictly, an IMU is *just* the accelerometer + gyroscope, reporting raw linear acceleration and angular velocity. It does **not** compute orientation on its own — that's left to whatever's reading it.
- **AHRS (Attitude and Heading Reference System)** — an IMU plus a magnetometer, plus onboard processing that fuses all three into a real orientation estimate (roll/pitch/yaw or a quaternion). **This is what the VN-100 is.** The "AHRS" name is the accurate one; people (including datasheets, including us in casual conversation) often just say "IMU" for convenience even when they mean an AHRS.
- **INS (Inertial Navigation System)** — a step up again: an AHRS (or bare IMU) plus algorithms that also track **position and velocity** over time by continuously integrating motion, not just orientation. This is a much harder problem than orientation alone (see the double-integration drift issue in section 9), so a standalone INS is usually only trustworthy over short periods without help from an outside reference.
- **GPS/INS** — an INS that periodically corrects its drifting position estimate using GPS fixes. Not applicable to us — GPS doesn't work underwater. This is exactly *why* AUVs use a DVL for the equivalent job GPS does on a plane or car: it gives a periodic, drift-correcting velocity reference so the accumulated position estimate doesn't wander forever between fixes. See `docs/sensors/dvl.md` once that's written.

**Where the VN-100 sits in this lineup:** it's an AHRS, not an IMU (though it contains one) and not an INS. It reports orientation confidently and continuously, but has no concept of position or velocity at all — that's what our EKF, and eventually the DVL, are for.

**Raw output (per the datasheet):**
- Full 3D orientation, accurate to about 0.5° in roll/pitch and 2.0° in yaw (heading), continuous across the entire 360° range — the sensor never has a "blind spot" or discontinuity as it rotates
- Angular velocity, gyroscope range up to ±2000°/s
- Linear acceleration, up to ±16g
- Update rate: up to 800 Hz for raw IMU data, up to 400 Hz for the fused attitude solution — far faster than our EKF actually needs, which currently runs at ~39 Hz output from our driver

**ROS2 message type:** `sensor_msgs/Imu`, published on `/vectornav/imu`

**Where it goes:** Subscribed to directly by `auv_localization`'s EKF node (`ekf_node`, from the `robot_localization` package). Only orientation and angular velocity are currently fused in — see section 9 for why linear acceleration is deliberately excluded for now.

**What breaks without it:** No orientation reference at all. Every attitude-related PID loop (roll, pitch, yaw) has nothing to control against, and the EKF loses its only current input entirely — right now, this is the *sole* sensor feeding the state estimator.

## 4. Software setup

### Prerequisites

ROS2 Humble installed (see `docs/jetson-setup.md`). Requires `python3-serial` (installed via apt, not pip — see below).

### Why we use a custom driver instead of VectorNav's official one

VectorNav's official ROS2 driver communicates with the sensor in **binary mode** — a compact, configurable packet format that lets it selectively request dozens of different data fields. In our testing, this driver would connect successfully and report the sensor's identity correctly, but never reliably produced a continuous stream of parsed, published data on our hardware — the underlying binary handshake didn't behave consistently for us, for reasons we weren't able to fully pin down.

The VN-100 has a second, much simpler output mode: plain human-readable **ASCII** text sentences (the sensor streams lines like `$VNYMR,...` continuously by default, straight out of the box, with no configuration needed at all). This mode worked reliably and consistently in every test we ran. Rather than continuing to debug the binary handshake, we wrote a small custom driver (`vn100_driver`, this repo) that reads these ASCII sentences directly and republishes them as a standard ROS2 `sensor_msgs/Imu` message.

**Trade-off worth knowing:** our custom driver only parses the `$VNYMR` sentence, which carries orientation, magnetometer, accelerometer, and gyroscope data — not the sensor's full binary-mode capability (raw uncompensated data, GPS/INS fields, etc.). We don't currently use any of those extra fields, so this hasn't cost us anything in practice, but it's worth knowing if a future need arises for data outside what `$VNYMR` carries.

### A quirk of the physical hardware: two ports, only one matters

The USB breakout exposes **two separate serial devices** to the OS (both generic FTDI chips) — only one of them actually carries the sensor's data stream; the other appears to be an unused secondary channel on the interface board. We identified the correct one empirically (by listening to each port directly) and locked it in permanently with a **udev rule** keyed to that specific port's unique hardware serial number. This means the correct port is always available at the fixed path `/dev/vectornav`, regardless of which raw `/dev/ttyUSB*` number Linux happens to assign it on a given boot (that assignment can and does change).

### Where the code lives

- Package path: `vn100_driver/` (this repo)
- Node: `vn100_driver/vn100_driver/vn100_ascii_node.py`
- Launch file: `vn100_driver/launch/vn100.launch.py`
- Mounting correction: a `static_transform_publisher` node, defined in `auv_bringup/launch/bringup.launch.py` (not inside this package — see section 8)

### Install / build

```bash
sudo apt install python3-serial
cd ~/auv_ws
colcon build --packages-select vn100_driver
```

### Key config parameters

Set directly as launch parameters in `vn100_driver/launch/vn100.launch.py`:

| Parameter | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `port` | `/dev/vectornav` | Serial device path (the stable, udev-locked name — see above) |
| `baud` | `115200` | Serial baud rate (the VN-100's factory default; we never changed it) |
| `frame_id` | `vectornav` | TF frame this sensor's data is stamped with — see section 8 |

## 5. Running it

```bash
ros2 launch vn100_driver vn100.launch.py
```

**What this command does:** Opens the sensor's serial port, continuously reads and parses `$VNYMR` sentences, and publishes each one as a `sensor_msgs/Imu` message on `/vectornav/imu`.

### Verify it's working

```bash
ros2 topic hz /vectornav/imu
```

**Expected output:** A steady rate around 39 Hz. If this prints "does not appear to be published yet" or shows no rate at all, see Troubleshooting below.

```bash
ros2 topic echo /vectornav/imu
```
**Expected output:** Continuously updating `orientation` (as a quaternion — not directly human-readable, but should visibly change if you rotate the sensor by hand) and `angular_velocity` fields.

### Visualizing / debugging

For a human-readable roll/pitch/yaw readout instead of raw quaternion numbers, there's a small standalone debug script (not part of the package — just a scratch tool) that subscribes to the topic and prints live degrees. Ask whoever last worked on this sensor if it's still saved somewhere, or it's quick to rewrite: subscribe to `/vectornav/imu`, convert the quaternion to Euler angles, print on a loop.

## 6. Calibration

- **Does this sensor need calibration?** The datasheet notes heading accuracy depends on "proper magnetic declination, suitable magnetic environment, and valid hard/soft iron calibration." We have **not yet performed magnetometer (hard-iron/soft-iron) calibration**.
- **Procedure:** Not yet done. This should be performed once the sensor is in its true final mounting position, physically near the vehicle's actual motors/thrusters/ferrous hardware — magnetic interference calibrated on an open bench won't reflect the real electrical environment the sensor will actually operate in.
- **How often to redo it:** At minimum, once after final integration; likely again if thrusters or major metal components are added/moved nearby afterward.
- **How to tell it's out of calibration:** Yaw (compass heading) reads inconsistently, drifts unexpectedly, or doesn't match a known real-world heading when checked by hand. Roll/pitch are less affected by this, since they don't depend on the magnetometer.

## 8. Coordinate frames

- **TF frame name:** `vectornav`
- **Parent frame:** `base_link`
- **Offset:** roll ≈ -178°, pitch ≈ -3°, yaw = 0° (translation: none measured/needed)
- **How this was measured:** Physically, empirically — with the sensor in its real final mounting orientation, placed on a surface believed to be level, and reading its live roll/pitch output directly. The ~178° roll offset is consistent with the sensor being mounted upside-down (see section 3); the small residual (2°, 3°) reflects imperfect leveling of the mount itself rather than a meaningful bias.

**Why a transform, not a code fix:** Rather than baking this correction into the driver's math, it's implemented as a standard ROS2 **static transform** (`vectornav_mount_tf`, defined in `auv_bringup`'s launch file), which tells `robot_localization` "here's how the sensor's own frame relates to the vehicle's frame." `robot_localization` automatically looks this up and rotates the data before fusing it. This keeps the driver publishing the sensor's honest, unmodified reading, and means if the mount ever changes, only this one transform needs updating — not driver code.

**If the sensor is ever remounted:** this offset must be re-measured and the transform's `--roll`/`--pitch` values updated to match. An out-of-date transform here would silently feed the EKF (and therefore every attitude PID loop) an incorrect orientation.

## 9. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `ros2 topic hz /vectornav/imu` shows nothing / "does not appear to be published yet" | Node not running, wrong port, or something else already has the serial port open | Check `ros2 node list`; check `sudo fuser -v /dev/vectornav` for a competing process (e.g. a leftover `minicom` session or an orphaned node from a previous crashed launch) |
| Raw sensor output looks like garbage / non-ASCII noise when checked directly (e.g. via `cat /dev/vectornav` or `minicom`) | Sensor is sitting in binary output mode rather than its default ASCII mode — can happen if VectorNav's official driver was run against it previously, even in an earlier session, since that mode can persist until a full power cycle | Fully unplug and replug the sensor's USB cable to power-cycle it back to its ASCII default; confirm with `minicom -D /dev/vectornav -b 115200` before relaunching ROS |
| Occasional single corrupted/garbled line in an otherwise clean data stream | Minor serial noise, not unusual over USB — the driver's checksum validation silently discards bad lines rather than passing them through | Not a concern at low, occasional rates. If it becomes frequent, check the physical USB cable/connection quality first |
| Fused `/odometry/filtered` orientation is way off (e.g. reads near 180° when the vehicle is level) | The static mounting transform (section 8) doesn't match the sensor's actual physical orientation — likely because it was remounted without updating the transform | Re-measure roll/pitch on a level surface (section 8) and update `vectornav_mount_tf`'s values in `auv_bringup`'s launch file |
| `/odometry/filtered` position/velocity grows without bound over time, even with the vehicle stationary | Raw linear acceleration is being fused into the EKF (`imu0_config`'s last row) with no independent position/velocity sensor yet available to keep the double integration in check | Set the acceleration row of `imu0_config` in `auv_localization/config/ekf.yaml` to `false` until a real position/velocity source (depth sensor, DVL) is wired in |

## 10. Testing & validation

### Bench test (do this before every pool session, or after any change to this sensor)
- [ ] Confirm `ros2 topic hz /vectornav/imu` shows a steady rate (~39 Hz)
- [ ] Rotate the sensor by hand on each axis (roll, pitch, yaw) and confirm the corresponding value in `/odometry/filtered` changes in the expected direction, and the other two axes stay roughly stable
- [ ] With the vehicle sitting level and stationary, confirm `/odometry/filtered` roll and pitch both read close to 0°
- **Sanity-check range:** At rest, roll/pitch should sit within a couple degrees of 0° (small deviation is normal mounting imperfection, not a bug); yaw is meaningless in absolute terms without a known heading reference, but should hold steady, not drift, while the vehicle isn't rotating.

### Pool test checklist items relevant to this sensor
- [ ] Confirm USB connector remains secure and dry after being handled/submerged
- [ ] Re-run the bench test checklist above after any transport/handling, before trusting attitude-hold in water

## 11. Maintenance & known limitations

- **Consumables / wear items:** None specific to this sensor beyond general USB connector wear.
- **Known limitations:**
  - Magnetometer-based yaw is vulnerable to magnetic interference from nearby motors/thrusters/ferrous metal — not yet calibrated for our final electrical environment (see section 6).
  - Our custom driver only parses the `$VNYMR` ASCII sentence — GPS, INS, and raw uncompensated data fields the sensor is capable of are not currently exposed to ROS2.
  - No NED→ENU axis conversion is performed inside the driver itself (VectorNav's native convention differs from ROS's standard). This is fully compensated for by the static mounting transform (section 8) for our specific mounting orientation — if the sensor is ever used in a way that bypasses that transform, this would need to be revisited.
- **Failure modes seen so far:**
  - The sensor can be left in binary output mode by other software (e.g. VectorNav's official driver) and will silently stay there across restarts — resolved by a full power cycle (unplug/replug), see Troubleshooting.
  - USB-level communication errors were observed during initial bring-up; resolved with a different cable/port and were not seen to recur once the sensor had a stable direct connection. Worth revisiting if similar symptoms reappear, especially once this sensor shares power/bandwidth with other USB devices (see the team's broader USB-hub-vs-powered-hub discussion).
